Research

My PhD research with Rishidev Chaudhuri at UC Davis characterizes chaotic recurrent neural networks, in the computational-neuroscience sense (not deep-learning RNNs). My current project compares how two stabilizing mechanisms shape sensitivity to perturbation (full Lyapunov spectra, attractor dimension, entropy) across network size and gain, against a baseline random network. A manuscript is in preparation.

Lyapunov spectra of three recurrent network models

Dynamical models of recurrent neural networks are highly useful tools for understanding the relationship between network connectivity and the resulting firing rate dynamics. The simplest version of this is a linear network without feedforward input, which can produce a limited range of dynamical behaviors and has closed-form solutions. Introducing nonlinearity generates more complex dynamics, including chaos, which emerges when sufficiently strong connections between neurons exist.

A classical example of this is a firing-rate model with connection weights randomly sampled from a Gaussian distribution and a tanh activation function. Sompolinsky, Crisanti, and Sommers (1988) characterized this model’s transition to chaos by varying a gain parameter g that controls the overall strength of connections. For a network of N neurons, the dynamics are

τ dxi/dt = −xi + g Σj Wij tanh(xj),

with weights drawn with mean zero and variance 1/N. Above a critical value of gain, a bifurcation occurs where the dynamics transition from stable to chaotic; in the large network limit, it occurs precisely at the critical gain value gc = 1. This random network model has served as a key benchmark in many further studies of recurrent network dynamics, because its transition to chaos can be characterized analytically using dynamical mean field theory. Lyapunov spectra

Chaos is defined as sensitive dependence on initial conditions, when arbitrarily small perturbations grow exponentially. Lyapunov exponents measure the rate of divergence between one trajectory initialized at x0 and another trajectory at a nearby point x0 + Δx0. When the maximal exponent λ1 > 0, the trajectories are diverging away from each other exponentially, and when λ1 < 0, they are converging to a stable attractor.

However, this quantity alone does not offer any information about the direction in phase space in which this separation or collapse is occurring. In N-dimensional phase space, there are many more directions of exponential divergence, described by the full Lyapunov spectrum λ1 ≥ λ2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ λN. Characterizing full spectra offers much more information beyond determining whether chaotic dynamics exist. I calculate several derivative quantities from the full spectrum:

  • The Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy rate H, a rate of information generation observed in chaotic systems, estimated by summing all positive exponents (Pesin’s identity).
  • The Kaplan–Yorke dimension D, which estimates the dimensionality of a chaotic attractor: k + (λ1 + ⋯ + λk)/|λk+1|, where k is the largest index such that the cumulative sum of exponents is non-negative.
  • The count of positive exponents n+, a measure of the number of independent directions of expansion.

I focus on population-normalized variants of all quantities (H/N, D/N, n+/N) to understand per-unit average contributions to the chaotic dynamics.

How the exponents are computed

The full spectrum is numerically estimated following the standard iterative Jacobian-based method developed by Shimada and Nagashima (1979) and Benettin et al. (1980). In the algorithm, an N × N system of orthonormal perturbation vectors is evolved alongside the trajectory using the Jacobian. A QR decomposition is periodically performed to reorthonormalize the perturbations. This procedure prevents collapse onto the dominant direction of exponential divergence, which would lose all information about an additional N − 1 independent directions. The Lyapunov exponents are estimated by accumulating the logarithms of the absolute values of the diagonal entries Rii of each QR decomposition and averaging over the total accumulation time. Chaos in the random network

The full Lyapunov spectrum of the classical random network model was characterized by Engelken et al. (2023). Its chaos is extensive, its spectrum is point-symmetric around −1/τ, and its attractor fills under 10% of the phase-space dimension. Three network models

The three network models considered here share the common goal of modeling firing-rate dynamics, but they differ in their connectivity structure and stabilization mechanisms. The classical random network provides a deeply-studied reference for studying the emergence of chaos by increasing recurrent connection strength. The dynamically critical (DC) network introduces a modification to this connectivity, with an extensive fraction of the eigenvalue spectrum placed on the edge of instability, and the Barcode network stabilizes recurrent activity with global inhibition and an activity-dependent leak. My analysis focuses only on autonomous dynamics in the absence of input. Panel B of the figure on the About page shows one network of each.

Dynamically critical networks are defined by their many dynamic modes on the edge of instability (Magnasco et al., 2009). I build one from the random connectivity by shifting approximately 30% of its eigenvalues to a real part of 0.99, keeping the eigenvectors and all imaginary parts. This model class is intentionally posed on the edge of a bifurcation where many dynamic modes will lose stability at once, unlike the randomly connected case, in which few modes lose stability at the initial onset of chaos. It is unknown how these networks behave after undergoing this bifurcation.

Barcode networks (Fang et al., 2025) have random normal weights, but use a ReLU activation function, global inhibition, and an activity-dependent leak that tracks overall network activity, a divisive normalization mechanism that stabilizes high activity dynamically. Due to the ReLU activation and dynamic leak, the origin is not an isolated fixed point as it was in the previous models, so the transition to chaos in the Barcode model cannot be described by an eigenvalue crossing alone.

I extend the Lyapunov spectrum analysis to these two additional classes of recurrent networks, to determine whether each variant of the architecture produces different signatures of chaos, while exploring the effects of network size and overall connection strength in each setting.

Study design and validation

I own this project end to end, from experimental design to analysis. I built a Python/PyTorch simulation pipeline with SLURM job arrays covering 1,060 parameter combinations for the three models, generating about 22,000 runs over nearly 90,000 CPU core-hours. For each combination of network size and gain, 20 network realizations are simulated, from N = 20 up to N = 2,000. Gain is varied from g = 0 to 300 for the Random and DC networks and from 0 to 120 for the Barcode network, sampled most densely near the empirically observed onset of chaos and the maxima of entropy and dimension.

Simulations are divided into three phases, to separate convergence of the trajectory to an attractor, convergence of the tangent basis, and longer-term accumulation of the stretching rates: a 500τ transient with only the network state evolved, a 100τ warm-up of the tangent basis, and accumulation of the exponents over 1000τ. Network dynamics are integrated by a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, and the tangent basis is advanced with the derivative of the discrete RK4 step, using closed-form analytic Jacobians for each model; these agree with automatic differentiation to within 10−15.

I adapted and extended a published Lyapunov-spectrum solver (Engelken et al., 2023), validated it against a reference implementation (Figure 4), and checked numerical convergence via paired comparisons of step size, reorthonormalization interval, duration, and precision.

Preliminary results

At matched maximum exponent λ1, the three networks can have different entropy and attractor dimension, and their spectra have clearly different shapes, so λ1 alone doesn’t tell the whole story. This is still being analyzed.
